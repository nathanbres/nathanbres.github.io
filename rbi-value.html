How It Works

RBI is the most intuitive run production stat, but it depends heavily on context: a hitter can only drive in the runners his teammates put on base. This analysis builds a per-plate-appearance rate, corrects it for the team's scoring environment, then compares it with what each player is paid.

The Indicator

RBI / PA: runs driven in per plate appearance. A rate, so part-time and full-time hitters are comparable. Minimum 300 PA.

Team Adjustment

Each player's rate is divided by a Team Context Index: the RBI/PA of his teammates relative to the MLB average, with his own production removed.

Salary

2026 salaries from Spotrac, on a log scale, with contract status (Pre-Arb, Arbitration, Free Agent) as context.

The Test

A linear regression of adjusted RBI/PA on log10(salary), run on all batters and within each contract status.

Model Methodology Team adjustment formula, worked example, regression results, and limitations ▼

The goal is to answer one question: how many runs does a hitter drive in per plate appearance, for what he is paid? Raw RBI totals reward playing time and strong lineups, so two corrections are applied: a per-PA rate, and a team context adjustment.

Formula

Team Context Index = [(Team RBI − Player RBI) / (Team PA − Player PA)] / MLB RBI per PA
Adjusted RBI/PA = (Player RBI / Player PA) / Team Context Index

The player's own numbers are removed from the team total before computing the index. Without that step, a great run producer would raise his own team's average and be penalized for it. For players who changed teams, the index is weighted by plate appearances with each club.

Worked Example: Kazuma Okamoto (TOR)

Raw RBI/PA92 / 636 = 0.14528% above the MLB average of 0.113
Teammates523 / 5,408 = 0.097Toronto without Okamoto: the weakest context in MLB
Context Index0.097 / 0.113 = 0.85515% fewer RBI chances than average
Adjusted RBI/PA0.145 / 0.855 = 0.169101 RBI per 600 PA, 8th of 263

Known Limitations

!Batting order is not corrected

The team adjustment handles the club, not the lineup slot. A leadoff hitter sees far fewer runners than a cleanup hitter. The BRS% section corrects for this.

!RBI is a context stat

It measures opportunity as much as skill. The BRS% section below addresses this by dividing by the actual runners on base.

!Salary is a single-year figure

2026 cash salary can understate a contract (signing bonuses, deferred money). Contract status is proxied by salary bands, not by actual service time.

!One season

A single year of RBI/PA is noisy. Four batters without a listed 2026 salary are excluded from the regression.

Contract status bands: Pre-Arb under $1M, Arbitration from $1M to under $7M, Free Agent $7M and above. The team adjustment changes the picture only modestly: raw and adjusted rankings correlate at 0.96.

Key Findings

The headline is a null result, and a useful one: on the 2026 season, salary tells you almost nothing about how efficiently a hitter drives in runs.

Salary Does Not Buy RBI Efficiency

Across 263 batters, the correlation between salary and adjusted RBI/PA is r = 0.07 (p = 0.28). Salary explains 0.4% of the variation. The result holds inside every contract status.

The Best Rates Come Cheap

Alec Burleson (STL, $3.3M) and Sal Stewart (CIN, pre-arb) lead MLB at 0.185 adjusted RBI/PA, about 111 RBI per 600 PA. Four of the top six earn under $1M.

Team Context Is Real but Modest

Run environments range from 0.90 (Toronto) to 1.11 (Cubs). The adjustment moves Andrés Giménez up 48 places and Christian Yelich down 57, yet the overall ranking barely changes.

Expensive and Low: Not Only a Role Effect

Trea Turner ($27.3M, 0.080), Jose Altuve ($30M, 0.084) and Mike Trout ($35.5M, 0.092) sit near the bottom. Lineup role explains part of it, but the BRS% section shows they also convert fewer of their chances.

Isolating Lineup Role: BRS%

The team adjustment fixes the club, not the batting order slot. To remove both at once, this section uses BRS%: the share of runners on base that a hitter drove in. A leadoff hitter who sees few runners is judged only on the runners he actually had.

Opportunity

Runners On per PA = BR / PA. How many runners were on base when the hitter came up. Driven by lineup slot and the hitters in front of him.

Conversion

BRS% = BRS / BR. How many of those runners he brought home. The skill part of run production, independent of role.

What It Leaves Out

The batter himself is not counted, so a solo home run adds nothing. Power hitters rank lower on BRS% than on RBI/PA.

Opportunity Varies a Lot

Runners on per PA ranges from 0.43 (JJ Wetherholt) to 0.76. Raw RBI/PA correlates at 0.34 with this number: a real share of RBI is handed to the hitter by his role.

Salary Still Explains Nothing

BRS% against salary: r = 0.00 (p = 0.94). Pre-Arb, Arbitration and Free Agent hitters all drive in about 14.5% of their runners, from the same number of chances.

Role Is Not the Whole Story

Turner (11.4%) and Kwan (10.4%) see few runners, but also convert well below the 14.1% average. Trout (9.6%) and Altuve (9.9%) rank 256th and 253rd of 263.

Two Separate Skills

Opportunity and conversion are almost unrelated (r = 0.09). Fernando Tatis Jr. sees few runners (0.47 per PA) yet drives in 18.0% of them, the best rate among $20M+ hitters.

So What?
The Market Does Not Pay for RBI, and It Is Probably Right

The original question was simple: how many runs does a hitter drive in for his salary? On the 2026 season the answer is that salary and RBI efficiency are unrelated (r = 0.07, p = 0.28). Free agents average 71 adjusted RBI per 600 PA at a median salary of $15.7M. Pre-arbitration players average 69 at $0.8M. Twenty times the money buys fewer than three extra RBI.

Removing lineup role with BRS% does not change the verdict: salary and the share of runners driven in are unrelated (r = 0.00). It does sharpen the reading of individual players. Trea Turner and Steven Kwan do see fewer runners at the top of the order, but they also convert well below average, and Mike Trout and Jose Altuve rank among the bottom ten of 263. For these four, the low RBI totals are not only a matter of role.

Where the indicator is useful: as a cheap screen for run producers in weak lineups. Kazuma Okamoto ($7M, Toronto), Vaughn Grissom (pre-arb, Angels) and Eugenio Suárez ($15M, Reds) all gain ground once their context is removed. The natural next step is to test whether BRS% is stable from one season to the next: if it is, it is a skill the market is not paying for; if not, it is mostly noise.

Glossary

RBI per PA vs. Salary
RBI
Runs Batted In. Runs that score as a direct result of a batter's plate appearance (hit, walk with bases loaded, sacrifice fly, and so on).
PA
Plate Appearances. Total times a batter comes to the plate, including walks and hit-by-pitches.
RBI/PA
RBI divided by plate appearances. How many runs a batter drives in per trip to the plate. League average in 2026: 0.113.
Team Context Index
RBI/PA of a player's teammates (team total minus the player's own RBI and PA), divided by the MLB average. Above 1.00 means a favorable run-scoring environment, below 1.00 an unfavorable one.
Adjusted RBI/PA
Raw RBI/PA divided by the Team Context Index. Removes the advantage or handicap of playing in a high or low scoring lineup.
RBI per 600 PA
Adjusted RBI/PA multiplied by 600, roughly a full season of playing time. Easier to read than a per-PA rate.
vs Expected
Adjusted RBI per 600 PA minus what the regression line predicts for a player at that salary. Positive means more run production than the salary suggests.
Pearson r
Correlation coefficient, from -1 to +1. Values near 0 mean no linear relationship between the two variables.
R²
R-squared. Share of the variation in the outcome explained by the model. 0.004 means salary explains 0.4% of the variation in adjusted RBI/PA.
p-value
Probability of seeing a relationship this strong by chance alone if there were no real link. Below 0.05 is the usual threshold for statistical significance.
Pre-Arb
Pre-Arbitration. Players in their first years of MLB service, earning near the league minimum. Proxied here by a 2026 salary under $1M.
Arbitration
Players with roughly 3 to 6 years of service who negotiate salaries through a formal process. Proxied here by a salary from $1M to under $7M.
Free Agent
Players on market-rate contracts. Proxied here by a salary of $7M or more.
BR (Baserunners)
Number of runners on base when the batter came to the plate, summed over all his plate appearances. Measures the run-driving opportunities he was given.
BRS
Baserunners Scored. How many of those runners scored as a result of the batter's plate appearance. Excludes the batter himself, so a solo home run adds nothing.
BRS%
BRS divided by BR: the share of runners on base that a batter drove in. Corrects for both lineup slot and team context, since it only counts the chances the hitter actually had. MLB average in 2026: 14.1%.
Runners On per PA
BR divided by PA. Average number of runners on base when the batter comes up. Low for leadoff hitters and players in weak lineups, high for middle-of-the-order bats.
2TM
Two teams. The player changed teams during the season; stats are combined and the team context is weighted by PA with each club.
